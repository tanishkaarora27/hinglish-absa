## A. Likely questions with model answers (46)

### Problem & motivation
1. **What problem does your project solve?** — It reads a Hinglish (Romanised Hindi-English) review of a quick-commerce app and tells, for each aspect such as delivery speed or product quality, whether the customer was positive, negative or neutral, flags sarcasm, and explains why. A star rating says *that* something is wrong; we say *what*.
2. **Why Hinglish and not English?** — A large share of Indian app reviews mix Hindi words in Roman script ("delivery bahut late thi"). English sentiment tools do not know *bahut* or *bakwas* and misread the review. We measured code-mixing with the Code-Mixing Index: mean CMI 17.1 in our data, and only 1.2% of reviews are monolingual.
3. **Why quick-commerce?** — It has a few clear, recurring aspects (10-minute promise, freshness, rider, fees, app), heavy review volume, and lots of sarcasm about the "10 minute" promise — a good test bed.
4. **What is aspect-based sentiment analysis?** — Instead of one sentiment per review, sentiment per *aspect* (feature of the product/service). "Fast delivery but expired milk" → delivery: positive, quality: negative.
5. **What is sarcasm and why does it matter for sentiment?** — Saying the opposite of what you mean, usually mock praise. "Great job, 2 ghante late 👏" reads positive to a bag-of-words model but is a complaint. Without handling it, positive counts are inflated.

### Data
6. **Where does the data come from?** — Public Google Play reviews of Blinkit, Zepto and Swiggy (Instamart), collected with the open-source `google-play-scraper` library; we keep only text, rating, date, app — no user names. We filtered to code-mixed reviews (≥10% Hindi tokens by our LID — about 8,500 of the scraped reviews) and labelled 2,500 of them, balanced across star ratings.
7. **Is scraping legal / ethical?** — Reviews are public and shown to everyone; we used the same public endpoint as the web page, no login, no bypassing, throttled requests, small research sample, no personal data stored, not redistributed. We also used two published research datasets under their licences (L3Cube-HingLID CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; Swami et al. 2018 corpus, GPL-3 repo).
8. **How did you label?** — A CSV sheet, one row per review, one column per aspect with pos/neg/neu or blank, plus a sarcasm flag; written guidelines with worked examples (`docs/01_labelling_guidelines.md`); the rule-based extractor pre-filled aspect guesses which were confirmed or corrected row by row.
9. **What is Cohen's kappa and why report it?** — Agreement between two annotators corrected for chance. It tells the examiner how noisy our labels are; sarcasm kappa is usually lower because sarcasm is subjective.
10. **What are the class distributions?** — 3,590 aspect instances: app/support 1,332, delivery speed 581, product quality 510, price 466, general 366, delivery partner 229, packaging 106. Sentiment: 1,960 negative / 1,485 positive / 145 neutral. Sarcastic: 17 reviews (0.7%). Negative dominates (people review when angry); neutral is the smallest class.
11. **Which public datasets did you use and for what?** — L3Cube-HingLID (word-level Hindi/English tags) to train our language-ID model; Swami et al. 2018 (5,250 tweets, 504 sarcastic) as a real benchmark for the sarcasm detector. SentiMix 2020 and ABSA-Mix are related work, not training data.
12. **Why not train on the published sarcasm tweets instead of labelling reviews?** — Domain shift: tweets about cricket and politics use different words and have no star rating; adding the 5,250 tweets to the training folds (transfer setting) did not improve review-level sarcasm F1 at all (table in section 7b). Our own labels are small but in-domain.

### Preprocessing
13. **What does normalisation do?** — Lowercase, Unicode-normalise, turn emoji into word tokens, collapse elongations (`soooo`→`soo`) while counting them, map ~300 spelling variants and slang to one form (`bkwas, bakwaas → bakwas`), merge phrases (`delivery boy → delivery_partner`), keep `!`/`?` as tokens.
14. **Why keep two copies of an elongated letter instead of one?** — "goood" and "good" stay slightly different (mild emphasis signal for char n-grams), while "goood" and "gooooood" become identical.
15. **Why convert emoji to words instead of deleting them?** — Emoji carry sentiment (😡, 👏) and are strong sarcasm cues (a clap next to a complaint). As tokens they become features.
16. **How does your language identification work?** — Each word → character n-grams (1–4) → TF-IDF → logistic regression, trained on 200K tagged tokens from L3Cube-HingLID. Accuracy 96.3% on its test split and 91.6% on a completely different corpus (Swami et al.). Numbers, emoji and placeholders are tagged `other` by rule; a few very short function words are overridden from a dictionary.
17. **What is the Code-Mixing Index?** — CMI = 100 × (1 − tokens of the dominant language / all language tokens). 0 = monolingual, 50 = perfectly balanced two-language mix. We report its distribution and use it as a sarcasm feature.
18. **Why character n-grams instead of words?** — Hinglish spelling varies; "bakwas", "bakwaas", "bkwas" share most of their 3-grams, so the model generalises across spellings that the map did not cover. Word n-grams are added for phrases like "bahut late".

### Aspect extraction
19. **How do you find aspects?** — Seed lexicon: ~30 trigger words/phrases per aspect (delivery_speed: late, der, jaldi, 10 min…; price: mehnga, charges, fee…). Rules: longest phrase first, every trigger becomes a mention with a position, reviews with no trigger get aspect `general`.
20. **Why a lexicon and not a learned aspect tagger?** — With ~2K reviews a sequence tagger would be data-starved; the lexicon works from day one, is fully transparent, and we *measure* it: micro-F1 0.758 against gold aspects (precision 0.73, recall 0.79; best aspect price F1 0.84, worst the `general` fallback 0.50), and we improved it once from the data (v1 0.687 → v2 0.758 by replacing generic triggers with phrases).
21. **What is its weakness?** — Coverage: an unseen word ("mirchi" for product) is missed; and implicit aspects ("it arrived warm" → quality) are missed. We report the miss rate and fall back to full text for those gold aspects during training.
22. **How did you build the lexicon?** — Hand-written from domain knowledge, then extended from the notebook's list of frequent out-of-lexicon tokens in our data.

### Models
23. **What are your features?** — Character 2–5-gram TF-IDF (word-boundary aware), word 1–2-gram TF-IDF, and 7 lexicon counts inside the window (positive/negative hits, negators, intensifiers, max scores, length). Sub-linear TF scaling.
24. **Which classifiers and why three?** — ComplementNB (good with little data and imbalance), Logistic Regression (probabilities, interpretable weights), Linear SVM (typically strongest for text; wrapped in a calibration layer to get probabilities). Three baselines show the result is not an accident of one model. Best: Logistic Regression, accuracy 0.880, macro-F1 0.717 ± 0.036 (ComplementNB 0.716; Linear-SVM 0.639 because it almost never predicts neutral).
25. **How do you handle class imbalance?** — `class_weight='balanced'` for LR/SVM (each class contributes equally to the loss) and balanced sample weights for NB; we optimise and report **macro-F1**, not accuracy.
26. **What is TF-IDF?** — Term frequency × inverse document frequency: common-everywhere n-grams get low weight, distinctive ones high weight.
27. **What is CalibratedClassifierCV and why do you need it?** — Linear SVM gives a distance to the hyperplane, not a probability; the calibration wrapper fits a mapping to probabilities using internal CV. We need probabilities for LIME, thresholds and the demo's confidence.
28. **What is stratified group k-fold cross-validation?** — Data split into 5 folds; *stratified* keeps class proportions similar in each fold; *group* keeps all windows of the same review in one fold so the model never sees part of a test review during training (leakage).
29. **Why not a simple train/test split?** — With ~2K instances a single split has high variance; 5-fold CV uses all data for testing once and gives a mean ± std.

### Sarcasm
30. **How do you detect sarcasm?** — A separate review-level classifier with TF-IDF plus 20 *sentiment-incongruity* features that measure the clash between praise words and a bad situation: both positive and negative lexicon words present, strongest-positive × strongest-negative, mock-praise markers (wah, great job, 👏) next to a negative word, emoji polarity vs text polarity, 1–2-star rating with positive words, elongation/`!`/CAPS counts.
31. **What is incongruity?** — The mismatch between literal and intended meaning that defines sarcasm. Shah & Maurya (2021) showed it is under-used in code-mixed sarcasm work; we make it explicit and countable.
32. **How good is it?** — On the public Swami et al. corpus (real, 10% sarcastic): LR F1 0.79 for the sarcastic class, equal to the original paper's 78.4%; incongruity features added +0.7 F1 there (tweets have no ratings). On our reviews we cannot measure it meaningfully: only 17 of 2,500 reviews are sarcastic, so 5-fold CV has ~3 positives per fold and the best F1 is 0.17 (LR, text + incongruity). We say that openly: sarcasm is rare in app reviews; the public benchmark is the reliable measure of the detector.
33. **What is polarity correction?** — If the review is predicted sarcastic and an aspect's sentiment is positive, we flip it to negative. Neutral/negative stay. Ablation: with predicted flags end-to-end macro-F1 stays 0.717 (the detector fires on almost nothing); with *gold* sarcasm flags (upper bound) accuracy on the 19 sarcastic-review instances rises from 0.684 to 0.895 — the correction rule is right, detection is the bottleneck.
34. **What if sarcasm is detected wrongly?** — A genuinely positive review gets flipped — the cost of a false positive. That is why we show the probability and let the threshold be tuned (sidebar slider); on the public benchmark precision is 0.68 at recall 0.95, so false positives are the main risk and the threshold slider matters.
35. **Why not detect sarcasm per aspect?** — Sarcasm is a property of the reviewer's tone for the whole review in almost all our examples; per-aspect labels would be too sparse to learn. Future work.

### Evaluation
36. **Which metrics and why?** — Precision, recall, F1 per class; macro-F1 as headline (classes are imbalanced, neutral and sarcastic matter); per-aspect F1; confusion matrix; aspect-extraction P/R/F1; ablations; error analysis.
37. **What is macro-F1?** — F1 computed per class then averaged with equal weight, so a rare class counts as much as a frequent one.
38. **What do the ablations show?** — A) windows vs full text: on all instances full text 0.735 vs K=5 window 0.717 — most reviews are single-topic, so cutting context only loses words; on the 2,022 multi-aspect instances windows win 0.612 → 0.653 — they help exactly where they were designed to. K=3 is too small (0.694); removing the 7 lexicon features costs 2 points. B) sarcasm correction: 0.717 → 0.717 with predicted flags, sarcastic-subset accuracy 0.684 → 0.895 with oracle flags. C) sarcasm feature sets: text / incongruity / both for three classifiers.
39. **Give one real error and its cause.** — "are also not of good quality" (product_quality, gold negative) predicted *positive*: *good quality* dominates and the negator *not* is only a count feature; a negation-scope rule would fix it. Another: "the product price is less compare to other" (gold positive) predicted *negative*: *less* is implicit praise for price but a negative word elsewhere.
40. **What is the confusion matrix telling you?** — 431 of 3,590 windows are wrong (12%); most confusion is neutral ↔ polar (neutral is 4% of data), and positive ↔ negative swaps come from negation, implicit sentiment and mixed clauses.

### Advanced concepts & explainability
41. **Explain aspect-conditioned windows.** — For each aspect mention we cut ±K tokens (K=5), stop at clause breakers (*but, lekin, par*) and at another aspect's trigger, and merge repeated mentions. The classifier input depends on the aspect asked → "conditioned". Example: "delivery fast thi | but | doodh expired tha".
42. **How did you choose K?** — Ablation K ∈ {3, 5, 8} vs full text; K=5 and K=8 are close (0.717 / 0.720) and both beat K=3 (0.694); we kept 5 as the smaller, more aspect-specific window; too small loses the sentiment word, too large leaks neighbouring aspects.
43. **How does your explanation work?** — For linear models the class score is b + Σwᵢxᵢ, so each feature's contribution is wᵢ·xᵢ; we rank and show them — exact, instant. Globally we list the top-weighted features per class. LIME is the second method: it perturbs the text, queries the model ~300 times and fits a local linear model to give word-level weights — model-agnostic but approximate and slower.
44. **What is LIME's limitation?** — Random perturbations → slightly different explanation each run; local only; slow for many queries. That is why the demo defaults to exact contributions.

### Practical use
45. **How would a company use this?** — Nightly batch-score all new reviews, aggregate % negative per aspect per city/week, alert on spikes (e.g. packaging complaints after a packaging change), route sarcastic 1-star reviews to support. Lexicons are config files the ops team can extend.
46. **How fast is it?** — Training: seconds. Prediction: ~100 reviews/second on one CPU core, including LID and explanations. The model file is 5.4 MB.

## B. Tough cross-questions, follow-ups, and strong answers (16)

1. **"Your lexicon-based aspect extraction is not machine learning."** — Correct, and intentional: with ~2K reviews a learned tagger would be worse than a measured lexicon; the ML is in sentiment and sarcasm. *Follow-up: "Why not at least a classifier for aspects?"* — We can: treat it as multi-label classification over the review; we did not because the lexicon already reaches micro-F1 0.758, and errors are mostly coverage, which a classifier with the same 2K reviews would not fix. It is listed as future work with an active-learning loop.
2. **"Isn't a ±5 window arbitrary?"** — It is a hyper-parameter; we tuned it by ablation (3/5/8/full) and the multi-aspect subset shows why it matters. *Follow-up: "What about long-range references?"* — They are a known failure case; a dependency parser for Hinglish does not exist, which is why we chose clause breakers as the boundary.
3. **"Only 2K labelled reviews — is that enough?"** — For linear models with char n-grams, yes: our 5-fold CV std is only ±0.036 macro-F1, and the three classifiers agree within a point. We are honest that rare aspect×neutral cells are thin, and we report per-aspect support.
4. **"Why should I believe a synthetic demo dataset?"** — You should not; the notebook tags every number from it `[DEMO-SYNTHETIC]` and the app shows a banner. All numbers on the slides are from the real labelled data; the public L3Cube and Swami numbers are from real published corpora.
5. **"Your sarcasm detection on reviews basically does not work — why keep it?"** — Because the problem is data, not method: 17 positives cannot train or test anything. The same detector reaches F1 0.79 on 5,250 real tweets, and the oracle ablation shows the correction logic raises sarcastic-subset accuracy from 0.68 to 0.90 when detection is right. So the module is validated; what is missing is in-domain sarcastic data — the deployed model is therefore trained on reviews + the Swami corpus + 20 hand-written seed examples, and we report that transparently. *Follow-up: "Isn't adding hand-written examples cheating?"* — It would be if they were evaluated on; they are training-only augmentation for a <1% class, excluded from every number, and documented in the notebook.
6. **"Sarcasm is subjective — how do you know the labels are right?"** — We wrote a rule ("mark 0 when unsure") and did a second targeted pass over all 1–2-star reviews containing praise words; still, we treat sarcasm as a minority class with a tunable threshold. We never claim certainty; the app shows a probability.
7. **"Why flip positive to negative — could sarcasm be positive?"** — In product reviews mock praise about a bad experience is overwhelmingly the case; 76% of our 17 sarcastic reviews carry a 1–2-star rating. Mock complaint ("terrible, I ordered 5 times this week") exists but is rare; we did not see enough to model it.
8. **"How is this different from the IIT Patna ABSA-Mix paper?"** — They convert English SemEval reviews into Devanagari Hinglish semi-automatically and train GPU deep models; their real set is 272 aspect terms from Zomato. We use real Roman-script reviews from a new domain, explicit sarcasm correction, CPU linear models and exact explanations.
9. **"Would a transformer beat you?"** — Probably by a few points of macro-F1 with a GPU and more data — SentiMix 2020 showed BERT-style models at the top but with modest margins over linear baselines. We kept a transformer row optional (notebook §12, frozen multilingual embeddings + LR on the same folds) and can show it; the trade-off is explainability, CPU time and data size. *Follow-up: "Then why claim your method is good?"* — Because the task constraints are CPU-only and small data; under those, char-n-gram linear models are the right tool, and the novelty is in windows, incongruity features and correction, which transfer to any classifier.
10. **"Did you leak information in cross-validation?"** — No: StratifiedGroupKFold with review_id as the group; the LID model is trained on a different corpus; TF-IDF vocabularies are fit inside each fold by the pipeline.
11. **"Your LID uses an external dataset with a non-commercial licence."** — Yes, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 — fine for an academic project and credited; for commercial deployment one would retrain on a permissively licensed corpus or own data.
12. **"Why does 'no window' beat windows on some rows of your ablation?"** — On single-aspect reviews the window *is* the review, so there is no gain, and cutting can drop a far-away sentiment word. The gain is on multi-aspect reviews, which is exactly the case windows were designed for; we show both tables.
13. **"How do you handle negation?"** — Negators (*nahi, mat, never, not…*) are counted inside the window as a feature and bigrams like "fresh nahi" are word features; we do not do explicit scope flipping. The error analysis shows negation errors at ~20% of inspected mistakes; a scope rule (flip the sign of the next sentiment word) is a planned improvement.
14. **"Why TF-IDF and not word embeddings / fastText?"** — No pretrained Roman-Hinglish embeddings ship with scikit-learn; training fastText needs a big corpus; char n-gram TF-IDF gives most of the same robustness to spelling and keeps features explainable. L3Cube's HingFT embeddings are a possible future comparison.
15. **"What breaks your system?"** — Devanagari script (not handled), Hinglish with very different romanisation (south-Indian styles), sarcasm without any praise word, implicit aspects, reviews about a competitor, very long reviews with many aspects.
16. **"Can I type a review right now?"** — Yes — the app is live; if the aspect word is not in the lexicon I will show you the one-line addition in `lexicon.py`.

## C. Limitations — and how to acknowledge them confidently
Say each as "known limitation → what we did about it → what we would do next".
| **Sarcasm is rare (17 / 2,500)** | "We found this out by labelling: 0.7% of Play Store reviews are sarcastic. We did not hide it — we benchmarked the detector on a public corpus, showed the oracle upper bound, and augmented training with public tweets + seed examples. Collecting more sarcastic reviews (e.g. from X/Reddit) is the clear next step." |
| Limitation | Honest acknowledgement |
|---|---|
| **Data size (~2K)** | "Small by deep-learning standards, adequate for linear models; we report CV std and per-aspect support, and the pipeline re-trains in seconds when more labels arrive (active learning next)." |
| **Label noise** | "Single-pass labelling with written guidelines and a pre-filled sheet; no second annotator, so we report no kappa and treat neutral/sarcastic as uncertain classes with probabilities instead of hard claims. A second annotator on 200 rows is the first thing we would add." |
| **Sarcasm ambiguity** | "Even humans disagree; we optimise the sarcastic-class F1, expose the threshold, and show evidence so a human can overrule." |
| **Domain dependence** | "Lexicons and labels are quick-commerce specific; the *method* (windows, incongruity, correction) is domain-independent — porting means a new 200-word lexicon and relabelling." |
| **Lexicon coverage** | "Measured: aspect recall 0.79, precision 0.73; the notebook lists frequent out-of-lexicon tokens so coverage grows with data." |
| **Spelling variation** | "A 300-entry map plus char n-grams; still, unseen romanisations hurt — the LID cross-corpus drop from 96% to 92% quantifies that." |
| **Synthetic demo data** | "Exists only to prove the code runs; every output from it is tagged; no slide uses it." |
| **No Devanagari** | "Out of scope by design (Roman script is dominant in app reviews); a transliteration step is the natural extension." |

## D. One-line answers to "Why this method and not deep learning?"
* "Two thousand labelled reviews and a CPU: that is the regime where char-n-gram linear models win on cost and often tie on accuracy."
* "Every prediction in our demo comes with exact feature contributions — a transformer cannot give that."
* "We could run 20+ ablation configurations in one 5-minute notebook; that is how we *know* windows and incongruity help."
* "The published state of the art on Hinglish sarcasm (XLM-R, 0.91 F1) needed GPUs and 166K tweets; the lab constraint rules that out, so we kept transformers as an optional, documented comparison."
* "Our contribution is problem structure — aspects, windows, incongruity, correction — which plugs into any classifier, including a transformer later."
